## The four profiles

- **Toll manufacturer.** The principal (usually the parent or brand owner) supplies the raw materials and detailed specifications; the factory just processes them into a finished or semi-finished product for a fee. It never owns the inventory, never makes production decisions, and carries essentially no market or inventory risk. Expect a small, very stable fee, typically a modest markup on processing cost alone.
- **Contract (routine) manufacturer.** A step up: the factory buys and owns its own raw materials and inventory, and manages day-to-day production, but still builds to detailed specifications set by the principal, who usually also decides what gets made, in what volume, and for which markets. This is the most common profile for factories inside multinational groups. Expect a modest, fairly stable markup, priced via Cost Plus or TNMM.
- **Licensed manufacturer.** The factory uses licensed technology, know-how, or patents from the group (usually paying a royalty for the right to do so), and has somewhat more autonomy in how production is run, though the underlying IP still belongs to whoever licensed it out. Margin sits a bit higher than a routine contract manufacturer's, reflecting the extra operational responsibility, but the royalty payment is where the IP owner captures its share.
- **Fully-fledged (entrepreneurial) manufacturer.** Owns its own IP and production know-how, decides what to build and how, funds its own R&D, and carries real market and inventory risk if products don't sell. This is the profile that's entitled to residual profit (or residual loss), the flip side of carrying real entrepreneurial risk.

| Profile | Owns inventory? | Owns IP? | Risk | Typical margin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Toll | No (principal supplies) | No | minimal | small, stable fee |
| Contract (routine) | Yes | No | some inventory | modest, stable markup |
| Licensed | Yes | licensed (pays royalty) | moderate | a bit higher |
| Fully-fledged | Yes | owns it | real market & inventory | residual profit / loss |

## Worked example

Meridian's Polish factory buys its own raw materials (aluminium housings, battery cells, circuit boards) and manages its own production floor, but builds strictly to specifications set by German headquarters, which decides product design, volumes, and which markets get supplied. It doesn't own any patents.

That profile, owns its inventory, but follows someone else's specifications and doesn't own the underlying IP, is a textbook **contract manufacturer**. It sits solidly in the middle of the spectrum: more exposed than a toll manufacturer (it does carry some inventory risk), but far short of fully-fledged (it has no say over what gets made or where it's sold, and owns no valuable IP of its own).

## Try it yourself

A separate Meridian entity in Vietnam produces a private-label charging cable for a large unrelated retail chain. Meridian Vietnam doesn't own the raw materials (the retail chain supplies them), doesn't own any IP in the product (a basic commodity cable), and is paid a fixed per-unit processing fee regardless of whether the retail chain successfully sells the cables or not.

1. Which of the four profiles best describes Meridian Vietnam for this specific arrangement?
2. What kind of margin would you expect it to earn?

<CollapsibleAnswer>
1. Toll manufacturer, it doesn't own the materials, follows a fixed process, and bears no market risk on the finished goods. 2. A small, stable processing fee, typically a modest markup on conversion cost only, reflecting close to zero risk.

## Key terms

- **Toll manufacturer:** processes materials supplied by the principal for a fee, owns nothing, bears minimal risk.
- **Contract (routine) manufacturer:** owns its own inventory and manages production, but builds to the principal's specifications and doesn't own the underlying IP.
- **Licensed manufacturer:** produces under a technology or IP licence from the group, paying a royalty, with moderate operational autonomy.
- **Fully-fledged (entrepreneurial) manufacturer:** owns its own IP, makes its own strategic production decisions, and bears real market and inventory risk.
