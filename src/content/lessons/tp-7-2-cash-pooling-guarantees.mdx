## Cash pooling

Instead of every subsidiary separately managing its own bank borrowing and deposits, a group can run a cash pool: a central treasury entity nets out each subsidiary's daily cash position, subsidiaries with surplus cash effectively lend to the pool, subsidiaries with a shortfall effectively borrow from it, and the group only needs to borrow or invest externally the net amount left over after pooling everyone's balances together.

This creates a genuine **synergy benefit**: pooled borrowing is usually cheaper than each entity borrowing separately, and pooled deposits often earn a better rate than scattered small balances would individually. The transfer pricing question is how that saved cost gets divided. A pool leader that simply pockets the entire benefit, paying participants a below-market deposit rate and charging them an above-market borrowing rate, is capturing value that arguably belongs to the group members whose cash actually created the saving in the first place. Arm's length practice generally expects the synergy benefit to be shared reasonably among participants, with the pool leader compensated only for the treasury function it actually performs (which is often a fairly routine service, priced with Cost Plus).

## Financial guarantees

When a parent formally guarantees a subsidiary's external borrowing, the subsidiary can typically borrow at a lower rate than it would achieve on its own credit standing, because the lender's risk is reduced. That reduction in borrowing cost is real economic value, and the guarantor is generally entitled to a fee for providing it.

The standard way to size a guarantee fee: estimate what the subsidiary would have paid to borrow without the guarantee (its standalone rate), estimate what it actually pays with the guarantee in place, and the difference, the interest saving the guarantee created, is the pool of value to be priced, with the guarantee fee typically set at some portion of that saving.

An important limit, echoing Lesson 7.1: if a subsidiary would already borrow cheaply due to implicit group support even without a formal guarantee, the guarantee's incremental benefit, and therefore its fee, is smaller than if the subsidiary would otherwise be a genuinely weak standalone credit.

## Try it yourself

Meridian's cash pool nets €40,000,000 in daily balances across five subsidiaries. Standalone, those subsidiaries would collectively pay an estimated €600,000 more per year in external borrowing costs and earn €150,000 less in deposit interest than they do through the pool, a combined annual synergy benefit of €750,000. The pool leader currently keeps the entire €750,000 and pays participants nothing beyond its own routine treasury fee.

## Key terms

- **Cash pooling:** netting multiple group entities' cash balances to reduce external borrowing and improve deposit returns.
- **Synergy benefit:** the cost saving created by pooling, which should be shared among participants rather than captured entirely by the pool leader.
- **Guarantee fee:** compensation paid to a guarantor for the real, measurable reduction in borrowing cost its guarantee provides.
