In January 2018, Danone shut its dairy plant near Delhi.

For most companies, that would have been the end of the India chapter. For Danone, it was the third ending. One of the biggest food companies in the world had now tried to win India three different ways, and walked away three times.

Exactly twelve months later, it was back. Not with a factory. Not with a new brand. With a cheque, and a small seat at the table of a five-year-old Mumbai startup that was trying to get Indians to eat Greek yogurt.

I spent a few weeks digging into that decision for a venture capital class at EMLV in Paris. What I found was a story about patience, control and a very human founder. And it answers a question most finance students never get a straight answer to: why would a giant with billions in the bank write a small cheque to someone else's company?

This is that story.

<h2 id="giant-losing-game">1. A giant that kept losing the same game</h2>

Danone is the company behind Activia, Evian and Alpro. It sells in more than 120 countries. And for 25 years, India kept beating it.

The first try was a partnership. In 1993 Danone teamed up with Britannia, one of India's best-known biscuit makers. It looked perfect on paper: a global dairy expert and a local brand every Indian household knew. But the partners ended up fighting over brands and rules, and in 2009 Danone sold its 25% stake for about $200 million.

The second try was drinks. Through two joint ventures with the Rahul Narang Group, Danone sold Qua, a premium mineral water, and B'lue, a flavoured water. In 2015 Danone left India's drinks market completely and shut both brands.

The third try was going solo. Danone sold its own yogurts and dairy products. But dairy never became more than about 10% of its Indian sales. Baby food and nutrition were carrying the business. So in January 2018, the dairy plant closed.

Three routes. A partner, a joint venture, a solo business. Three exits.

Then, on 16 January 2019, the Indo-French Chamber of Commerce ran a headline that must have raised a few eyebrows in Mumbai.

<h2 id="yogurt-founder">2. The man who sold India a yogurt it didn't know it wanted</h2>

To understand why Danone came back through Epigamia, you have to understand the person who built it.

Rohan Mirchandani studied at NYU and Wharton and worked in finance in New York. He could have stayed. Instead he came back to Mumbai and, in 2013, started an ice cream brand called Hoki Poki. In 2015 he made a stranger bet: Greek yogurt.

Think about how strange that sounded at the time. Indian homes already had curd. Many families make it at home, for very little. And here was a startup asking people to pay more for a thick, high-protein yogurt in a small plastic cup, in fruit flavours.

It worked. Epigamia did not just sell a product, it created a shelf. By 2019 it was in more than 10,000 stores.

<h2 id="why-small-cheque">3. Why would a giant write a small cheque?</h2>

Here is the part most people get wrong about big companies and startups.

Big companies are great at running what already works. They are slow at finding what will work next. A new product has to pass research, committees and budget cycles. Startups are the opposite: fast, cheap, willing to fail. So the giant wants to sit close to new ideas, without betting the whole company on them.

That is what corporate venture capital is for. A normal venture fund takes money from many outside investors and has one job: make a return. A corporate venture fund uses one company's money and has two jobs. Make a return, and help the parent learn, enter a market or find its next acquisition.

That second job is the key to this whole story.

Danone's fund is called Danone Manifesto Ventures. It launched in New York in 2016 with about $150 million, and by 2022 it had put more than $300 million into over 20 food startups. Its style is easy to describe: buy a small stake in a healthy food brand, help it grow, keep the option to buy the whole thing later.

So when you hear "corporate VC", picture a big company buying a front-row seat to a market, before deciding whether to buy the theatre.

<h2 id="january-2019-deal">4. January 2019: the deal</h2>

In January 2019, Epigamia raised Rs 182 crore, about $22 million. It was a Series C round, which is a useful detail. Seed money backs an idea. Series A backs a first product. By Series C, the business already sells and the money is for growing faster. So Danone was buying into a brand that already worked.

The round was co-led by Danone Manifesto Ventures and Verlinvest, a Belgian investor that had backed Epigamia since its $6 million Series A in 2016. And Danone chose to stay small. A minority stake, on purpose.

> "This is a minority venture investment, so Epigamia will continue to operate independently."
>
> *Laurent Marcel, then head of Danone Manifesto Ventures, IFCCI, January 2019*

A few months later, in May 2019, Bollywood actor Deepika Padukone invested too and became the face of the brand. A French food giant and one of India's biggest film stars, both betting on Greek yogurt in the same year.

One honest gap. Danone never said how much of the Rs 182 crore was its own money. I can show you the size of the round, but not the size of Danone's cheque. Keep that in mind when anyone gives you a confident number.

<h2 id="hard-years">5. The hard years</h2>

Startups rarely grow in a straight line, and Epigamia did not either.

In 2023, milk prices in India jumped. For a yogurt company, milk is the main cost, so every price rise eats straight into margins. According to Reuters, the founders even held talks about selling the business. Instead, they shelved the sale, cut costs and pushed for profit.

It paid off. In FY24, revenue was Rs 173.7 crore and the net loss fell 74%, from Rs 67 crore to Rs 17.4 crore. In December 2023, the company was valued at about Rs 1,250 crore.

<h2 id="founder-cut-short">6. A founder's story, cut short</h2>

Then came news that had nothing to do with finance.

In December 2024, Rohan Mirchandani passed away after a cardiac arrest. He was 42.

His co-founder Ankur Goel stepped in as acting head and kept the company running. In May 2026, Epigamia named Ritesh Gauba, a consumer goods leader from Mars, Pladis and Britannia, as its new CEO, and Ankur Goel became co-founder and COO. The company reported more than 50% growth in FY26, with better profitability.

<h2 id="playbook-door-closed">7. The playbook, and the door that closed</h2>

Remember the second job of a corporate fund. At some point, the parent may want to own the whole thing.

Danone had done this before. In 2017 its fund bought a minority stake in Harmless Harvest, an American coconut water brand. In 2021 it took majority control. Minority first, control later. That is the playbook.

<SourceLink

With Epigamia, it looked like the same move was coming. In May 2025, reports said Danone was exploring raising its stake from about 30% to 60%, which would have given it control.

It did not happen.

In August 2026, Inc42 reported a $20 million secondary deal. Secondary means existing shares changed hands and no new money went into the company. Verlinvest and the Mirchandani family bought more, and a new investor, Sauce.vc, joined. The sellers included DSG Consumer Partners and Deepika Padukone's KA Enterprises. Danone was not named as a buyer.

So did Danone make money? Honestly, we cannot say. The fund does not publish its returns, and its current stake is not public. What we can say is that the company is worth far more than in 2019, and the door to control, for now, is closed.

<h2 id="what-danone-got">8. What Danone actually got</h2>

If you judge this deal only on money, you miss the point. So let me judge it on the second job.

Danone got a low-risk way back into Indian dairy after three failures. It got a close view of how young Indian shoppers buy, from a founder who understood them far better than any head office in Paris. And it kept an option on a brand that might one day be worth buying.

Epigamia got something too. Not just money, but know-how. Factories, food safety, scaling, logistics. Things a big dairy company has done a thousand times and a startup is doing for the first time.

The best part is that this is not only Danone's version. The founder said it himself.

> "Strategic advice in terms of production strategy, scaling up, logistics and operations."
>
> *Rohan Mirchandani on Danone's support, Just Food interview, September 2020*

When both sides of a deal describe the same benefit, you can believe it. On strategy, the bet worked.

<h2 id="esg-claims">9. Good for people and planet? Checking the claims</h2>

Every company says it cares. The useful question is who else has checked.

On paper, Danone is one of the strongest companies in the world on ESG, which means environment, social and governance. In June 2020 it became the first listed company to adopt "société à mission" status in France. That means its mission, bringing health through food, is written into its bylaws and checked by an independent committee. In November 2025, the whole group became a certified B Corp, a label audited by an outside nonprofit, B Lab.

<h2 id="the-warning">10. The warning Danone had already lived through</h2>

There is one more story that makes the Epigamia question sharper, and it happened in Paris.

Emmanuel Faber was Danone's CEO and the face of "purpose first". He is the man who pushed the mission status through. In March 2021 he was pushed out after pressure from two activist investment funds, Artisan and Bluebell. Their reason was simple. From July 2018 to March 2021, Danone's share price fell 2% while Nestlé's rose 32%.

> "None of them were opposed to what we were doing."
>
> *Emmanuel Faber, TIME, November 2021*

So the investors had no problem with the mission. Their problem was the results. When mission and money clash, shareholders tend to win.

And the same tension sits inside the Epigamia deal. Is Danone a patient investor helping a founder's company grow? Or a big company waiting for the right moment to take over?

<h2 id="did-the-bet-work">11. So, did the bet work?</h2>

My answer is: partly. It was a smart way back into India, with one question still open.

- **Strategy: the clearest win.** After three failures, Danone is back in Indian dairy at low risk, and the founder confirmed the help was real.
- **Money: promising but unproven.** The company is worth far more, made its first profit and cut losses by 74%. But there is no exit and no published return.
- **ESG: the weakest link.** Verified at Danone level. Not measured at Epigamia level.

The biggest tension is between two roles. A venture investor lets the founder grow. A corporation wants control. In 2025 Danone explored control. In 2026 Epigamia's other owners chose to double down instead.

So my one-line lesson: corporate venture capital works best when the corporation is patient.

## What I took away from this

**Finance is simpler than its vocabulary.** Corporate VC sounds complicated. It is basically a big company buying a front-row seat before deciding whether to buy the whole show. Once you see why it exists, the how follows.

**Stated is not verified.** Every company says good things about itself. The work is in checking which claims someone outside has confirmed. That one habit has changed how I read press releases and annual reports.

**The money is only half the deal.** Judged only on returns, this deal is unclear. Judged on what Danone was actually trying to buy, it clearly worked. Good analysis starts with the question: what did the investor want?
