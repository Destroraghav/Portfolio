## The legislative backbone

Luxembourg's domestic transfer pricing rules rest on two pillars: the general tax procedure law's cooperation obligations, and **Articles 56 and 56bis of the LITL**, which explicitly incorporate the arm's length principle into Luxembourg tax law and align it with the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines this course has referenced throughout (Module 1's Article 9 of the OECD Model Tax Convention is the international parallel to Luxembourg's own Articles 56/56bis). In practice, this means everything covered in Modules 1 through 10, comparability, the five methods, functional analysis, is directly applicable under Luxembourg domestic law, not just as international guidance.

## The intra-group financing circular

Because financing companies are such a common feature of Luxembourg structures, the Luxembourg tax administration issued specific administrative guidance addressing them directly (an administrative circular tied to Articles 56/56bis). Two ideas are worth knowing well:

- **Equity at risk.** A financing company that borrows from one group entity and on-lends to another isn't automatically entitled to keep the full spread between what it pays and what it receives, unless it's genuinely acting like an intra-group bank, with real capacity to bear credit risk. The guidance requires identifying how much "equity at risk" the financing company is genuinely exposed to, based on a credit risk analysis of the entity it's lending to, and pricing its remuneration based on that risk exposure, not simply on the size of the loan passing through it.
- **The routine intermediary benchmark.** For a financing company that functions more like a simple administrative intermediary than a real risk-bearing intra-group bank, the guidance sets out that its arm's length remuneration should generally correspond to a return of around 2% (after tax) on the assets it finances, unless the company prepares a full transfer pricing study under OECD methodology showing a different return is more appropriate.

## Worked example

Meridian's Luxembourg finance subsidiary, Meridian Finance S.à r.l., receives €20,000,000 in funding from Meridian AG (Germany) and on-lends the same amount to Meridian France for its warehouse expansion (first introduced in Lesson 1.1). A functional analysis (Module 1) shows Meridian Finance doesn't perform independent credit assessment, doesn't have discretion to reject the German parent's funding instructions, and has no capacity to absorb a loss if Meridian France couldn't repay, all of that risk effectively sits with the German parent. Meridian Finance is, in substance, a routine administrative intermediary.

If Meridian Finance is currently retaining a much larger spread than the benchmark, that's a strong signal the structure needs either a proper transfer pricing study justifying the larger spread based on real risk-bearing, or a repricing down toward the routine benchmark.

## Key terms

- **Articles 56 and 56bis LITL:** the Luxembourg Income Tax Law provisions codifying the arm's length principle domestically.
- **Equity at risk:** the portion of a financing company's capital genuinely exposed to credit risk, central to pricing its remuneration under Luxembourg's financing guidance.
- **Routine intermediary benchmark:** the administrative guidance's reference point of roughly 2% after-tax return on financed assets for financing companies that don't genuinely bear credit risk.
