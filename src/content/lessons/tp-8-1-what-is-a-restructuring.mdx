## A billion-dollar illustration of what's really at stake when functions move

<CaseCard title="Facebook v. Commissioner" year="US Tax Court, 2025" outcome="The court found the IRS's approach sound but its inputs wrong, and recalculated ~$7.8B, above Facebook's figure, far below the IRS's.">
In 2010, Facebook (the US parent) entered a cost-sharing agreement with its Irish subsidiaries, transferring the rights to exploit certain intangibles (the technology and systems underlying the platform) for use outside the US. Facebook Ireland owed a "buy-in" payment reflecting the value received. Facebook valued it at a net present value of **$6.3 billion**. The IRS argued the real value was roughly **$19.9 billion**, and litigation stretched on for years. The Tax Court ultimately settled on around **$7.8 billion**.
</CaseCard>

Set aside the exact mechanism (a cost-sharing buy-in is a US-specific concept, not identical to the "exit tax" this lesson leads into next). The underlying economics are exactly what this module is about: when a US company hands a foreign subsidiary the rights to exploit years of valuable technology and future growth, real value is leaving the origin country's tax base, and tax authorities everywhere have a strong interest in making sure that transfer is priced honestly, not treated as if it happened for free or for a token sum.

## Why restructurings matter so much in transfer pricing

A restructuring changes a company's functional profile, often converting it from something closer to full-fledged (bearing real risk, owning real assets, making real decisions) to something closer to routine (Module 5's spectrum, applied here to distribution or other functions rather than just manufacturing). The classic pattern: a local distributor that used to negotiate its own contracts, hold its own inventory, and carry real credit risk gets converted into a limited-risk distributor, with headquarters taking over pricing decisions, absorbing inventory risk through a buy-back guarantee, and centralising strategic functions.

If that conversion happens with no compensation to the local entity, the group has effectively moved future profit potential, and the ability to earn a higher, more volatile margin, out of that country for free. An independent business, giving up a valuable function, a profitable customer relationship, or a stream of future income, would never walk away from that without compensation, and neither should a related entity, at least not without the transaction being examined closely.

## Worked example

Meridian France, established across Module 1 as a full-fledged-leaning distributor with real marketing functions and real inventory risk, is being restructured: headquarters will take over pricing decisions, provide a buy-back guarantee on unsold inventory, and absorb credit risk on unpaid retailer invoices going forward. Meridian France becomes a limited-risk distributor overnight.

Before the restructuring, Meridian France's functional profile justified an operating margin in the 3.1%–5.05% range established in Module 3. After the restructuring, it will look more like a routine LRD, with a benchmark range closer to 1.5%–2.5%. That's a real, ongoing reduction in Meridian France's expected future profitability, driven entirely by giving up functions and risk it used to carry.

Under OECD guidance, Meridian France would generally be entitled to compensation reflecting the value of what it's giving up, potentially the value of a terminated or substantially renegotiated arrangement, calculated based on what an independent distributor would have been paid to walk away from a similarly valuable, ongoing business relationship.

## Key terms

- **Business restructuring:** a cross-border reorganisation of functions, assets, or risks between related parties.
- **Profit potential:** the future income-earning capacity associated with a function, asset, or risk, which restructuring rules require be compensated for if transferred away.
