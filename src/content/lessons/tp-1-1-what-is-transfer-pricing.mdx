## The case that shows why this matters: McDonald's in Luxembourg

In 2009, the Luxembourg tax authorities gave McDonald's two private tax rulings. The setup was simple on paper: a McDonald's company registered in Luxembourg, called McD Europe Franchising, collected franchise royalties from restaurants across Europe and Russia. Under the first ruling, Luxembourg agreed not to tax those royalties, because the company had a branch in the United States, and under the Luxembourg-US tax treaty, profits earned through a US branch are taxed in the US instead.

Except the US never taxed them either. The branch didn't meet the US definition of a taxable presence, so American authorities had no claim on the income. Luxembourg found out, and in a second ruling in 2012, decided the exemption still applied anyway. The result: several hundred million euros in franchise royalties fell into a gap between two countries' tax systems and were never taxed by either.

The European Commission opened a formal investigation in December 2015 to check whether this was illegal state aid, meaning Luxembourg had quietly given McDonald's a special deal unavailable to anyone else. Three years later, in September 2018, the Commission concluded it wasn't. Luxembourg had simply applied its own tax law and its treaty with the US correctly. The double non-taxation was a byproduct of the two countries' rules not lining up, not a secret favor. It was the first case of its kind where the Commission's investigation ended without a finding of illegal aid, which made it notable in its own right.

Compare that to Apple's arrangement in Ireland, investigated around the same period. There, the European Commission found that Ireland had allocated the vast majority of Apple's European profits to a head office that existed only on paper, with no staff and no real activity, letting Apple pay a fraction of the tax it should have. After years of appeals, the EU's top court confirmed in September 2024 that this was illegal aid, and ordered Ireland to recover roughly 13 billion euros from Apple.

Same continent, same era, two multinational companies structuring how profit moves between their entities. One was found to be an abuse of a mismatch in the rules. The other was found to be an actual sweetheart deal. The dividing line between them is exactly what this course is about: how a group is allowed to price what one part of itself sells to another part, and how you tell the difference between a defensible price and an aggressive one.

## So what exactly is a transfer price?

A multinational enterprise (MNE) is just a group of companies under common ownership, operating in more than one country. Picture a simplified group: a parent company in Germany that designs and owns the brand for wireless headphones, a factory in Poland that assembles them, and a sales company in France that sells them to retailers. We'll follow this group, which we'll call **Meridian Group**, throughout the course.

When the Polish factory sells finished headphones to the French sales company, someone has to put a price on that shipment, even though both companies belong to the same parent and nobody is really "negotiating" in the way two strangers would. That internal price is the transfer price.

The OECD, the international body whose guidelines most countries follow, defines transfer prices as the prices at which a company transfers goods, intangible assets, or services to enterprises it's associated with. Any transaction that fits that description is called a **controlled transaction**, because unlike a sale between two unrelated companies, the terms aren't shaped by open market negotiation. They're set internally, by the group itself.

That's exactly why tax authorities pay attention. If Meridian's Polish factory sells headphones to the French sales company at an artificially low price, most of the profit from that headphone ends up sitting in France instead of Poland. If the price is artificially high, the opposite happens. Multiply that by every product, every service, every internal loan a group runs through its subsidiaries, and you can see how a group could quietly decide, transaction by transaction, which country gets to tax how much of its profit. Transfer pricing rules exist to stop that decision from being arbitrary.

## A small worked example

Say Meridian's Polish factory produces a pair of headphones for 40 euros in materials and labor. It sells that pair to Meridian France for 45 euros. Meridian France then sells it on to retailers for 90 euros.

- Poland's profit on the transaction: 5 euros (45 minus 40)
- France's profit on the transaction: 45 euros (90 minus 45)

If Poland's corporate tax rate is higher than France's, this pricing quietly shifts most of the taxable profit into the lower-tax country. Now imagine Meridian instead charged 70 euros for the same shipment: Poland's profit jumps to 30 euros, France's drops to 20. Nothing about the product changed. Only the internal price did, and with it, where the tax bill lands.

Neither number is automatically "wrong." The whole discipline of transfer pricing is about figuring out which price an independent factory would actually have charged an unrelated buyer for the same headphones, under the same conditions. That's the benchmark we'll build up to over the rest of this module.

## Key terms

- **Multinational enterprise (MNE):** a group of companies under common ownership operating in more than one country.
- **Transfer price:** the price set for a transaction between two entities that belong to the same group.
- **Controlled transaction:** any transfer of goods, services, financing, or intangible assets between associated enterprises, as opposed to an "uncontrolled transaction" between two independent parties.
