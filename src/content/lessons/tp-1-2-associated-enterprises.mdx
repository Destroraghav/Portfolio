## Why this question comes first

Before you can ask whether a price is fair, you need to know whether the two parties are even required to price things at arm's length in the first place. Two strangers can charge each other whatever they want. Two companies in the same group can't, but only if the law recognizes them as being "in the same group" to begin with. That's what this lesson is about.

The OECD Model Tax Convention, in Article 9, sets the international baseline: two enterprises are associated if one participates directly or indirectly in the management, control, or capital of the other, or if the same people control both. Most countries build their domestic transfer pricing rules on top of that idea, and it splits into two tests.

## Test 1: Legal dependence

This is the intuitive one. If Company A owns more than a set threshold of Company B's shares, commonly 50%, A controls B, and the two are associated. Ownership can be direct (A holds the shares itself) or indirect (A owns a holding company that owns B).

But legal control isn't only about the ownership percentage. A minority shareholder can still have legal control in substance through a **veto right**: the contractual power to block key decisions, like approving the annual budget, hiring the CEO, or authorizing major investments. If an investor holds only 30% of a company's shares but can single-handedly block its budget every year, that investor has real influence over the company's decisions, and most tax authorities will treat the two as associated regardless of the ownership percentage.

## Test 2: Economic dependence

This is the one people miss. Two companies can have zero shared ownership and still count as associated, if one is economically dependent on the other. A few classic patterns:

- **Customer concentration.** A supplier that sells 90% or more of its output to a single buyer, under a contract that lets the buyer dictate prices and terms, is arguably not operating as an independent business anymore, even without a single share changing hands.
- **Financial dependence.** If Company A survives only because Company B extends it interest-free financing that covers most of its costs, and A would collapse without it, tax authorities can treat that financial lifeline as a form of control.
- **Common management.** If the same individuals sit on the boards or hold executive roles at two companies with no shared ownership, and major decisions for both get made by that same small group of people, the two can be treated as associated.

The theme across all three: tax law generally favors **substance over form**. What matters is who actually calls the shots and who actually depends on whom, not what the org chart or shareholder register says on paper.

## Worked example: Meridian Group's supplier

Meridian Group's Polish factory buys a specialized battery component from a small independent supplier, VoltCell, based in the Czech Republic. Meridian owns none of VoltCell's shares. But VoltCell sells 95% of everything it produces to Meridian, under a five-year exclusivity contract that also lets Meridian set the purchase price unilaterally each quarter.

Is VoltCell an associated enterprise of Meridian Group, even without a single share owned?

Walking through it: there's no legal dependence here, no ownership, no veto right. But there's a strong case for economic dependence: VoltCell's survival depends almost entirely on one customer, and that customer also controls the price. A tax authority reviewing this relationship would likely conclude the two are associated in substance, which means any transactions between them fall under transfer pricing rules even though VoltCell is, on paper, a fully independent company.

## Key terms

- **Associated enterprises:** two or more entities linked through legal control or economic dependence, and therefore subject to transfer pricing rules on their transactions with each other.
- **Legal dependence:** control established through share ownership or contractual rights like a veto over key decisions.
- **Economic dependence:** control established in substance, through customer concentration, financial reliance, or shared management, without formal ownership.
- **Substance over form:** the tax law principle that actual economic reality takes priority over how a relationship is structured on paper.
