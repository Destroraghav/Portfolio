## Why exit taxes exist

Imagine a company has built up a valuable, unrealised gain inside a country, an appreciated asset, a valuable piece of IP, or growing future profit potential tied to functions based there, and then simply relocates that asset, function, or its own tax residence to another country, often a lower-tax one, before that value is ever taxed. Without a specific rule, the country of origin would lose its right to tax value that was substantially built up under its jurisdiction, purely through a paper relocation, with no genuine sale ever taking place to trigger a normal tax event.

Exit taxes close that gap: they treat the departure itself as a taxable event, generally requiring the company to pay tax on the built-up gain at the moment of departure, as if the asset had been sold at fair market value right before it left, even though no actual buyer is involved. Within the EU, this is formalised through the **Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD)**, which requires member states to apply exit taxation in specific circumstances, including when a company transfers assets, its tax residence, or the business carried on by a permanent establishment, out of that member state's taxing jurisdiction.

## How this connects back to transfer pricing

Exit taxes and the restructuring compensation principle from Lesson 8.1 address the same underlying economic reality from two different legal angles. A well-advised restructuring has to satisfy **both** questions, not just one:

| | Transfer pricing question | Exit tax question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asks | Did the related party losing functions or assets get paid what an independent party would have demanded? | Does the country of origin get to tax the value leaving its jurisdiction? |
| Focus | Intercompany compensation | The origin country's own taxing right |
| Source | OECD Guidelines, Chapter IX | Domestic law (in the EU, ATAD Article 5) |

## Worked example

Meridian AG decides to relocate its central IP holding function, including the noise-cancellation patents built up in Germany over the past decade, to Meridian's Luxembourg entity, as part of a broader restructuring of how the group manages its intangibles. At the moment of transfer, the patents carry a substantial built-in, unrealised value reflecting years of German R&D investment and market success.

Two separate questions arise. First, the transfer pricing question from Lesson 8.1: did the German entity receive arm's length compensation for transferring intangibles of real, demonstrable value, using the methods from Module 6? Second, the exit tax question: do Germany's domestic exit taxation rules (implementing ATAD, since Germany is an EU member state) require Meridian to recognise and pay tax on the built-in gain in the patents at the moment they leave German tax jurisdiction, independent of whatever intercompany compensation was arranged? Both questions need a clean, defensible, well-documented answer, addressing only one of them leaves real exposure on the other.

## Key terms

- **Exit tax:** a tax charged on the built-in, unrealised value of assets, functions, or tax residence at the point they leave a country's taxing jurisdiction.
- **ATAD (Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive):** the EU directive requiring member states to apply exit taxation in specified circumstances.
